\begin{abstract}
We present a deterministic, CPU-friendly instance-segmentation pipeline for
solar filaments. The method removes center-to-limb brightness variation with
robust radial normalization, identifies dark filament candidates relative to
the visible solar disk, separates them into connected components, filters small
components, and emits compressed COCO run-length encodings. Our strongest
official Kaggle submission is platform reference \texttt{55113085}, with a
public score of \textbf{0.48}. This is higher than our later Mask R-CNN (0.31)
and frozen public YOLO plus U-Net (0.34) experiments. The classical pipeline is
therefore the selected final method. Its principal advantages are
reproducibility, low compute cost, and transparent failure modes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
This report describes our submitted solution to the Solar Filament
Segmentation Challenge 2026 \cite{competition}, organized by the Earth-Space AI
Research Lab. The challenge uses GONG H-$\alpha$ observations and evaluates
pixel-level masks for individual solar filaments with Panoptic Quality (PQ)
\cite{kirillov2019panoptic}. In addition to overlap, the evaluation penalizes
false detections, missed instances, fragmentation, and over-merging.

Solar full-disk images exhibit strong center-to-limb brightness variation. A
fixed global threshold therefore confounds the dark limb and background with
actual filament material. Our contribution is an intentionally simple,
inspectable correction and grouping pipeline. We do not claim a new neural
architecture. Instead, we report the method that produced our highest verified
platform score, distinguish official scores from local checks, and provide an
end-to-end notebook that needs no weights or private supporting files.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
\subsection{Design objective}
The final system was chosen to optimize the complete competition objective, not
only binary foreground overlap.  A useful prediction must localize dark material,
partition it into plausible instances, avoid duplicate pixels across instances,
and serialize every mask in the required format.  These constraints favor a
small number of explicit stages whose effects can be inspected independently.
The resulting prediction path is summarized below.

\subsection{Input and radial normalization}
Official JPEG inputs are converted to grayscale. For an image of height $H$ and
width $W$, we use its geometric center and a disk radius
$r_d=0.48\min(H,W)$. Disk pixels are divided into 96 radial bins. For every bin
$b$, the background profile $P_b$ is its upper-quartile intensity. The upper
quartile is less sensitive than the mean to dark filament contamination. A
pixel $x$ in bin $b(x)$ is normalized as
\begin{equation}
  N(x)=\frac{I(x)}{\max(P_{b(x)},10^{-6})}.
\end{equation}
This operation removes broad radial illumination variation while preserving
localized dark structures. Pixels outside the estimated disk are never
considered filament candidates.

\subsection{Candidate extraction and instance generation}
Within the disk, we compute the 25th percentile $q_{0.25}$ of normalized
intensity and set
\begin{equation}
  \tau=\min(q_{0.25},0.95).
\end{equation}
Pixels satisfying $N(x)\leq\tau$ form the candidate foreground. Four-connected
component labeling converts this foreground into instances. During submitted
inference, components smaller than 20 pixels are removed and no more than the
64 largest components per image are retained. Accepted masks are encoded as
compressed COCO RLE \cite{lin2014coco}. Sorting input paths and component sizes
makes the output deterministic for fixed data and dependencies.

The complete prediction path is implemented in \path{solarfil/infer.py} and
\path{solarfil/segment.py}. The supplied final notebook installs the pinned
environment, finds the official test directory, runs every test image, writes
the required two-column CSV, checks unique identifiers and nonempty encodings,
and decodes a sample RLE to verify its dimensions. It performs no training,
downloads no model, and calls no inference service.

\subsection{Algorithm and invariants}
For each sorted input path, Algorithm~\ref{alg:inference} maintains a single
grayscale image, normalized array, Boolean candidate map, and integer label map.
The label map is reused to encode one selected component at a time, avoiding a
dense stack of up to 64 full-resolution masks.  Component identifiers are sorted
by decreasing area; Python's stable ordering provides deterministic tie handling
for a fixed SciPy labeling result.

\begin{figure}[h]
\small
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Read one JPEG as grayscale and estimate the circular disk.
  \item Compute 96 upper-quartile radial profile values and divide the image by
        the profile selected for each pixel.
  \item Compute $\tau$ from disk pixels only and threshold inside the disk.
  \item Label four-connected foreground and measure every component area.
  \item Discard components below 20 pixels; keep at most 64 by area.
  \item Encode each retained label as compressed COCO RLE and write its unique
        \texttt{<image>\_<rank>} identifier immediately.
\end{enumerate}
\caption{Per-image inference procedure implemented by the final notebook.}
\label{alg:inference}
\end{figure}

Three invariants are checked at the output boundary. First, each row has exactly
the two required fields. Second, identifiers are unique across the file. Third,
decoding a sampled RLE recovers the source image dimensions and a nonempty mask.
Connected components are disjoint by construction, so no pixel is assigned to
two predicted instances from the same image. These checks catch packaging and
serialization faults that a visually plausible overlay would not reveal.

\subsection{Why the final route is classical}
The three submitted routes answer different hypotheses. The radial method asks
whether photometric correction plus explicit topology is sufficient. Mask R-CNN
tests learned region proposals and instance masks. The public YOLO plus U-Net
route tests a detector followed by learned refinement. Only the first route
improved the entrant-owned public result to 0.48. We therefore use the public
leaderboard solely as a selection signal among completed submissions and do not
combine incompatible outputs after seeing their scores.

\section{Engineering validation}
\subsection{Synthetic tests}
Repository tests exercise the operations that are independent of unavailable
ground truth. Radial normalization is tested on synthetic disks containing dark
elongated regions. Component tests verify that disconnected regions remain
separate and that small regions are filtered. Submission tests check that mask
priority removes overlap, empty residual masks are dropped, and compressed RLE
round-trips exactly. Metric tests verify that duplicate predictions reduce PQ,
which guards against accidentally optimizing only matched-mask Dice.

Tests for the final deliverables parse the notebook as version-4 notebook JSON,
require the complete inference and validation cells, and reject dependencies on
the experimental computer-vision stack. They also assert exact version pins and
check that the report preserves all three entrant submission references and the
score-provenance warning. These are contract tests: they do not estimate hidden
leaderboard performance, but they make drift between code, notebook, and report
visible before submission.

\subsection{Complexity and failure containment}
Let $n=HW$ and let $B=96$ be the fixed radial-bin count. Radius assignment,
profile lookup, thresholding, connected-component labeling, and RLE traversal
are linear in $n$. Computing quantiles requires additional selection work but
does not change the practical image-wise memory bound. Component ranking depends
on the number of labels, which cannot exceed $n$. The implementation deliberately
processes and releases one image at a time, so dataset size increases runtime but
not peak image storage.

A corrupt or unsupported file fails at its own read rather than contaminating a
learned state. A scene with no retained component emits no instance row for that
image; it does not manufacture a placeholder mask. A scene with excessive
fragmentation is bounded by the 64-instance cap. These choices make failures
observable and keep output size finite, although they can trade recall for
robustness on unusually complex images.

\subsection{Parameter roles and sensitivity}
The final configuration exposes four conceptually distinct controls. The disk
radius excludes off-disk background before any image statistic is calculated.
The radial-bin count governs how locally the illumination profile follows
center-to-limb variation: too few bins leave a residual gradient, while too many
make the profile vulnerable to local structures and sparsely populated outer
annuli. The darkness quantile controls foreground coverage, and its 0.95 ceiling
prevents the adaptive threshold from admitting pixels that are not dark relative
to their radial neighborhood. Minimum area and maximum instance count regularize
topology after thresholding rather than changing pixel contrast.

These parameters were kept fixed for the final submission. We do not present a
numerical ablation because no verified ablation table was recorded for the
submitted classical run. Retrospectively fabricating one would confuse design
rationale with measured evidence. A future controlled study should vary one
parameter family at a time on a fixed image-level validation split and report
both PQ and predicted-to-true instance-count ratio. The latter distinguishes a
plausible foreground mask from excessive fragmentation or merging.

\subsection{Submission-interface audit}
The competition interface is treated as part of the method. File discovery is
restricted to JPEG paths and sorted lexicographically, providing stable image
order without assuming filesystem enumeration order. Each identifier combines
the source stem with a one-based within-image rank. Masks are encoded through
the COCO API in Fortran array order and the byte representation is converted to
text before CSV serialization. The writer owns quoting and newline handling,
instead of concatenating fields manually.

The notebook validates the artifact after the writer closes it. It checks the
header, ensures that data rows exist, rejects duplicate identifiers, rejects
empty encoding strings, decodes a sample, and compares its shape to the source.
This cannot prove that every instance is correct, but it rules out common
zero-score failures: misspelled headers, duplicate keys, malformed RLE,
transposed dimensions, and output written to the wrong location. Keeping these
checks next to inference makes the notebook closer to the exact submitted path.

\section{Discussion}
Radial normalization is effective here as an inductive bias: solar observations
have a known disk geometry and a large smooth photometric trend, while filaments
are localized dark deviations. Encoding that structure explicitly reduces the
amount of evidence the algorithm must infer from labeled examples. The method is
also auditable because every accepted pixel can be traced to its radial bin,
threshold, and connected component.

Simplicity is not semantic understanding. Connectivity cannot decide whether
touching dark structures are distinct filaments, and it cannot bridge a faint
interruption belonging to one physical object. Learned models remain promising
when validated training data, reliable weights, and sufficient compute are
available. For this submission, measured entrant-owned scores favor the
transparent baseline, so the package reports that result rather than selecting
the most sophisticated model.

\section{Limitations and reproducibility}
Intensity connectivity is not a semantic model. A filament whose contrast is
interrupted may fragment into several components, while neighboring filaments
that touch after thresholding may merge. Bright active regions and acquisition
artifacts can distort the radial profile. A fixed geometric disk can be less
accurate than per-image limb estimation, and the minimum-area threshold can
discard true fine structures. Finally, public leaderboard performance may not
equal the final evaluation result.

The public release package comprises the MIT-licensed source, an exact pinned
requirements file, and the final classical notebook in the \texttt{kaggle}
directory. A reviewer attaches the official competition dataset and runs that
notebook from the repository root; it writes \texttt{submission-classical.csv}
to Kaggle's standard working directory without a checkpoint or untracked input.
Competition images and generated submissions are not redistributed in Git.

\subsection{Reproduction protocol}
The reviewer should begin in a fresh Kaggle session with the competition data
attached and Internet access disabled after dependency availability is
confirmed. Running the notebook from top to bottom performs five auditable
phases: environment installation, source import, dataset discovery, inference,
and output validation. The resulting CSV is written under the standard working
directory. The displayed row count, identifier uniqueness assertion, decoded
sample dimensions, and file preview should all complete before the artifact is
submitted.

The repository intentionally excludes competition imagery, generated CSV files,
and caches. Therefore a clean clone plus the organizer-provided dataset is the
reproduction boundary. The classical result does not depend on a private split,
checkpoint, secret, network service, or local absolute path. The final submitter
must still verify that Kaggle exposes the same directory layout and that platform
reference 55113085 corresponds to the 0.48 public score shown in this report.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
The main external-validity threat is leaderboard sampling. A public score is
computed on the platform's public partition and may reward choices that do not
generalize to the private partition. We mitigate this only by selecting a simple
method with physically motivated normalization and by reporting the public score
as public; we do not claim a private or final score. A second threat is dataset
shift across observatories, acquisition times, exposure settings, or disk sizes.
The fixed 0.48 radius and contrast thresholds are properties of this submitted
configuration rather than universal solar-image constants.

The principal construct-validity threat is that PQ combines recognition and
segmentation quality, whereas visual inspection tends to emphasize only pixel
coverage. A dark region can look reasonable while being split into too many
instances, which increases false positives, or while merging distinct filaments,
which increases false negatives. This is why our implementation tests duplicate
penalties and disjointness, and why future validation should record instance
count alongside overlap. Nonetheless, without organizer ground truth for the
test data, this report cannot diagnose which PQ component dominates the 0.48
result.

Reproducibility has two remaining environmental threats. First, Kaggle may
provide cached package wheels differently from a local Python environment;
pinned versions specify the target but do not guarantee offline availability.
Second, input directory names are platform-controlled. The notebook searches
for the expected test JPEGs and prints the resolved path, but the final submitter
must inspect that resolution rather than assume it. These are operational checks,
not reasons to modify the algorithm after score selection.

\subsection{Potential extensions}
Several extensions follow directly from observed limitations but are deliberately
left outside the submitted result. Estimating the limb per image could replace
the fixed disk radius. Morphological linking guided by local orientation could
bridge faint gaps without connecting all nearby dark pixels. A watershed or
graph-cut stage could split touching candidates using intensity ridges. Finally,
a learned instance model could use radial-normalized input as an additional
channel, retaining the photometric prior while learning shape cues.

Any such extension should be evaluated on a frozen image-level split before a
new platform submission. It should compare PQ, matched-mask overlap, false
positive and false negative counts, and predicted-to-true instance ratio. Model
weights and preprocessing would also need immutable hashes and a license audit.
These proposals are future work only; none contributes to reference 55113085 or
to the score claimed in this report.

AI assistance was used to help implement, test, and document repository
materials. No AI-generated number is presented as an evaluation result. The
entrant remains responsible for executing the notebook, verifying the three
platform references, understanding the method, and submitting the final
repository and report.

\section{Conclusion}
We present a complete, deterministic solar-filament instance-segmentation
pipeline based on radial photometric normalization and connected components.
Its strongest verified public score is 0.48 under platform reference 55113085,
above the two learned alternatives attempted by the entrant. The accompanying
notebook, pinned minimal environment, artifact checks, tests, and explicit score
provenance make the selected result straightforward to inspect and reproduce.
Remaining scientific limitations are chiefly instance merging, fragmentation,
and sensitivity to disk and contrast assumptions.
